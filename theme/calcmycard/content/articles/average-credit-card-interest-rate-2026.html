What "average APR" actually measures

The two Fed figures measure different things. "All accounts" is the stated APR averaged across every credit card account at reporting banks. "Accounts assessed interest" is total finance charges divided by the average daily balances they were charged on, and it leaves out accounts that paid no interest — which is why it usually runs higher. Advertised rates on new card offers are a third, separate measure and vary widely by card and credit tier. None of these tells you what you should be paying — only what a broad population is paying.

How the Fed's average compares with other sources

You'll see lower "average APR" figures quoted elsewhere, often around 19% to 20%. They aren't wrong; they measure something else. Most private trackers average the advertised APRs on card offers, while the Fed's 22.15% figure is what accounts that actually carried a balance were charged.

For judging your own rate, compare it with offers for your credit tier; for what card debt really costs the average borrower, the Fed's figure is the better guide.

Why rates have climbed over the past several years

Credit card APRs are usually structured as a benchmark rate (commonly the prime rate) plus a margin set by the issuer. When benchmark rates rise, most variable-rate credit cards reprice upward automatically, without any change to your individual creditworthiness. This is the main driver behind broad, industry-wide APR increases. To see what a Fed hike or cut does to your own card, use the Fed Rate Change Calculator.

How to tell if your specific rate is good, average, or high

Compare your card's purchase APR to current published averages for your general credit tier (see what counts as a good credit card interest rate) (excellent, good, fair, limited). A rate meaningfully above the average for your tier is worth challenging — either by calling your issuer to ask for a reduction, or by shopping for a new card or balance transfer offer better suited to your current credit profile.

What actually determines your individual rate

Card issuers price risk based on your credit score, income, existing debt levels, and the specific card product's target market (rewards cards, secured cards, and store cards often carry different baseline rates). Two people can qualify for the exact same card and receive different APRs within that card's advertised range.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will paying off my card lower my APR?

Not automatically — your APR is set by your account terms, not your current balance. However, a strong payment history over time can make you a better candidate for a rate-reduction request or a better balance transfer offer.

Do all my cards have the same APR?

No. Each card (and often each transaction type on the same card — purchases, balance transfers, and cash advances) can carry a different APR, even from the same issuer.
