Quick Answer

To calculate credit card interest by hand: divide your APR by 365 to get the daily periodic rate, multiply by your average daily balance, then multiply by the number of days in your billing cycle. The result is an estimate of the interest for that cycle — your issuer's statement calculation may differ.

You don't need special software to calculate credit card interest — just three numbers and a calculator (or this one). Here's a practical way to estimate credit card interest using the daily periodic rate and average daily balance, broken into steps you can follow yourself. Your issuer's actual method is set out in your cardholder agreement.

Step 1: Find your daily periodic rate

Take your card's APR (as a decimal) and divide by 365 — the number of days many issuers use per year (some use 360; check your cardholder agreement if you want an exact match). A 22% APR becomes a daily rate of about 0.0603%.

Step 2: Find your average daily balance

Add up your balance for every single day in the billing cycle, then divide by the number of days. If your balance was $1,000 for the first 15 days and $1,500 for the remaining 15 days of a 30-day cycle: (15 × $1,000 + 15 × $1,500) ÷ 30 = $1,250 average daily balance.

Step 3: Multiply it all together

Estimated interest = daily periodic rate × average daily balance × number of days in the cycle. Using the numbers above: 0.0603% × $1,250 × 30 days ≈ $22.61 in estimated interest for that cycle. Some issuers compound interest daily or treat certain balances differently, so this hand calculation is an estimate, and your statement may differ.

Where people usually get it wrong

The most common mistake is using your current balance instead of your average daily balance, which produces the wrong number whenever your balance changed during the cycle (which it almost always does). The second most common mistake is dividing by 12 instead of 365 — that gives you a rough monthly estimate rather than a daily-rate calculation.

When the "simple" monthly method is good enough

For planning and comparison purposes — deciding whether to pay extra, compare a balance transfer, or budget for a payoff — a simplified monthly rate (APR ÷ 12) applied to your current balance gives an easy-to-follow estimate. That's the method most of our calculators use because it's simple to check by hand. This monthly method is useful for planning, but it can differ materially from an issuer's actual statement calculation, since issuers can use daily periodic rates, average daily balances, fees, and card-specific terms.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I calculate this in a spreadsheet?

Yes — see our guide on building a credit card interest formula in a spreadsheet for a ready-to-use approach.

Why doesn't my manual calculation match my statement exactly?

A hand calculation is an estimate. Differences can come from daily compounding, a different day-count convention (360 vs. 365), the timing of purchases and payments within the cycle, fees included in the balance, grace-period rules, balances with different APRs, and rounding. Your statement and cardholder agreement describe how your issuer actually calculates interest.
