Quick Answer

To calculate credit card interest: divide your APR by 365 to get the daily periodic rate, multiply it by your average daily balance, then multiply by the number of days in your billing cycle. For example, $2,000 at 24.99% APR for a 30-day cycle is $2,000 × (0.2499 ÷ 365) × 30 ≈ $41.08. For a quick estimate, use balance × APR ÷ 12 instead.

The credit card interest formula

Daily periodic rate = APR ÷ 365
Interest for the cycle = Daily periodic rate × Average daily balance × Days in the cycle

Some issuers divide by 360 instead of 365, and most compound daily, which adds a few cents. Quick monthly estimate: Balance × APR ÷ 12.

Step 1: Find your daily periodic rate

Take your card's APR (as a decimal) and divide by 365 — the number of days many issuers use per year (some use 360; check your cardholder agreement if you want an exact match). A 22% APR becomes a daily rate of about 0.0603%; 24.99% becomes about 0.0685%.

Step 2: Find your average daily balance

Add up your balance for every day in the billing cycle, then divide by the number of days. If your balance was $1,000 for the first 15 days and $1,500 for the remaining 15 days of a 30-day cycle: (15 × $1,000 + 15 × $1,500) ÷ 30 = $1,250 average daily balance.

Step 3: Multiply it all together

Interest = daily periodic rate × average daily balance × days in the cycle. Using the numbers above at 22% APR: 0.0603% × $1,250 × 30 days ≈ $22.60 for that cycle. The monthly shortcut ($1,250 × 22% ÷ 12) gives $22.92, close enough for planning.

Is credit card interest charged daily or monthly?

Most issuers calculate it daily and bill it monthly: interest builds every day at the daily periodic rate and shows up as one charge on your statement. Many also compound daily, adding each day's interest to the balance, so the next day's interest is charged on a slightly higher amount. On the $2,033 example, daily compounding adds about 42 cents for the month ($42.18 instead of $41.76).

Where people usually get it wrong

The most common mistake is using your current balance instead of your average daily balance, which gives the wrong number whenever your balance changed during the cycle. The second is multiplying the balance by the full APR without dividing by 365 (or by 12), which overstates a month's interest about twelvefold. The third is forgetting that if you didn't pay last month's statement in full, new purchases may start accruing interest right away, because you can lose the grace period.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I calculate monthly interest on a credit card?

Multiply your balance by your APR and divide by 12. A $3,000 balance at 21% APR is about $3,000 × 0.21 ÷ 12 = $52.50 a month. For the exact figure your issuer charges, use the daily method above with your average daily balance and the number of days in the cycle.

Does credit card interest compound?

Usually yes. Most issuers compound daily, and unpaid interest becomes part of the balance that next month's interest is charged on. That's why a balance you don't pay down keeps growing even without new purchases.

Why doesn't my manual calculation match my statement exactly?

Use our Interest Charge Checker to compare your statement with the formula. A hand calculation is an estimate. Differences can come from daily compounding, a different day-count convention (360 vs. 365), the timing of purchases and payments within the cycle, fees included in the balance, grace-period rules, balances with different APRs, and rounding. Your statement and cardholder agreement describe how your issuer actually calculates interest.
