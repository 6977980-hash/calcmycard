Quick Answer

On a credit card, the APR is the interest rate. Unlike a mortgage APR, it doesn't bundle in upfront fees, so a 24.99% APR card charges interest at 24.99% a year, worked out as a daily rate of about 0.0685%. The number that differs is the effective annual rate: because most cards compound daily, 24.99% APR works out to about 28.4% a year if you carry the whole balance for 12 months.

On loans such as mortgages, APR and interest rate are two different numbers. On a credit card they mostly merge into one, but the APR still hides a second figure (the effective rate after daily compounding) and usually comes in several versions on the same card. The tables below put real numbers on both.

Why they differ on a mortgage but not on a credit card

A mortgage's APR spreads upfront costs (origination fees, points, some closing costs) over the loan term, so it sits above the note rate. Take a $300,000, 30-year loan at 6.50% with $6,000 in points and lender fees paid upfront: the monthly payment is about $1,896, but you only received $294,000, so the APR comes out at about 6.70%. Credit cards don't fold upfront fees into the rate this way. Annual fees, late fees and balance transfer fees are charged separately, so the card's APR and its interest rate are the same number.

APR vs. effective rate: what daily compounding adds

Many issuers divide the APR by 365 and charge that daily rate on your balance every day, adding each day's interest to the balance. If you carried the same balance for a full year without paying it down, the true yearly cost would be higher than the APR. The figures below use the Federal Reserve's latest averages and a few common card APRs.

Why one card lists several APRs

Most cards show more than one APR. Each works the same way, but applies to a different part of your balance and follows different rules.

The APRs on a typical card and when interest starts.
APR typeApplies toWhen interest starts
Purchase APRNew purchasesAfter the grace period, if you didn't pay the last statement in full
Balance transfer APRDebt moved from another cardUsually from the transfer date (often 0% for a promo period, plus a fee)
Cash advance APRATM cash and cash-like chargesUsually the same day, with no grace period, often at a higher rate
Penalty APRExisting balance after a payment 60+ days late; new charges after other triggersAfter the issuer's notice; on an existing balance only once you're 60 days late (Reg Z § 1026.55)

Payments above the minimum must go to the balance with the highest APR first, so a card carrying a cash advance gets the most benefit from paying extra.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a lower APR always better than a higher one with rewards?

It depends on whether you carry a balance. If you always pay in full, APR rarely matters and rewards can be the deciding factor. If you sometimes carry a balance, a lower APR is usually worth more: on a $3,000 balance, the gap between 21.99% and 27.99% is about $15 a month in interest.

Does APR include annual fees?

No. On a credit card, annual fees, late fees and balance transfer fees are separate charges. The APR applies only to interest on balances you carry.

Is the APR the same as the monthly rate?

No. The APR is a yearly rate. The monthly rate is about APR ÷ 12 (24.99% ÷ 12 ≈ 2.08%), and the daily rate many issuers actually use is APR ÷ 365 (about 0.0685%).
