Quick Answer

On a card with a grace period, the surest way to avoid interest on purchases is to pay your full statement balance by the due date every cycle. If you're already carrying a balance, a 0% intro APR card, a balance transfer, or a fixed-rate consolidation loan are the next-best ways to stop or reduce what you're paying.

Credit card interest isn't inevitable — it's a choice built into how you use the card, and it's avoidable with the right habits or the right financial move. Here are seven ways to stop paying it, roughly in order of how immediately available they are.

1. Pay your statement balance in full, every cycle

This is the most reliable way to pay no interest on purchases, and it works because of the grace period many cards offer on new purchases. If you don't pay your statement balance in full, you may lose your grace period for new purchases, depending on your card's terms. To see what the gap costs over time, read pay in full vs. minimum payment.

2. Set up autopay for the full statement balance, not the minimum

Most autopay settings default to "minimum payment." Changing that single setting to "full statement balance" removes the risk of a missed or partial payment derailing your grace period by accident.

3. Use a 0% intro APR card for planned large purchases

If you know a big expense is coming, a 0% intro APR card lets you spread the cost over months without paying interest — as long as you pay it off before the promotional period ends. Use the 0% Intro APR Calculator to find the monthly payment needed to finish in time. Store-card offers that say "no interest if paid in full" work differently: miss the deadline and interest is charged back to the purchase date, which the Deferred Interest Calculator shows.

4. Move existing high-interest balances with a transfer

If you're already carrying a balance, a balance transfer to a 0% or low-APR card can eliminate most or all of the interest you'd otherwise pay, minus a one-time transfer fee. The Balance Transfer Calculator shows whether the fee is worth it for your specific balance.

5. Consolidate with a fixed-rate personal loan

For larger balances or longer payoff timelines, a fixed-rate loan can replace variable, high credit card interest with a predictable lower rate and a payment that doesn't change. Compare the two approaches with the Debt Consolidation vs. Balance Transfer Calculator.

6. Pay more than once per cycle

Because interest is based on your average daily balance, an extra mid-cycle payment (even a small one) lowers your average balance for the rest of the cycle and reduces that month's interest charge — even if your total monthly payment doesn't change.

7. Avoid cash advances entirely

Cash advances usually carry a higher APR than purchases and a separate fee, and according to the CFPB, interest on a cash advance generally starts as soon as you take the money out — there's no grace period. If you need cash, look for lower-cost alternatives first, and use our Cash Advance Calculator to see what one would really cost.

Two details that trip people up

Statement balance vs. current balance: which one to pay

Your statement balance is what you owed on the day your billing cycle closed; it's printed on your statement and doesn't change until the next one. Your current balance is what you owe right now, including purchases, payments and refunds that posted after the statement closed. To keep your grace period, you need to pay the statement balance in full by the due date. Paying the higher current balance also works, but it isn't required: purchases made after the statement closed have their own grace period and aren't due until the next statement.

Paying less than the statement balance is where interest starts. Federal rules (Regulation Z § 1026.54) stop issuers from charging interest on balances from earlier billing cycles, or on the part of a grace-period balance you repaid on time, but the unpaid part and new purchases can start building interest, depending on your card's terms. The grace period guide explains how that works.

Residual interest: the charge after you pay "in full"

If you were already carrying a balance, interest keeps adding up every day between your statement date and the day your payoff posts. That leftover, called residual or trailing interest, shows up on the next statement even though you paid the full amount you were shown. For example, $2,000 at 24% APR building interest for 20 days is about $26 ($2,000 × 24% ÷ 365 × 20). Pay that small charge in full by its due date and the interest usually stops, or ask your issuer for a payoff amount and pay that instead. How credit card interest works covers when interest starts and stops, and the Interest Charge Checker shows whether a charge on your statement adds up.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I ask my issuer to lower my APR?

Yes — it's a fair phone call to make, especially if you have a strong payment history. It doesn't always work, but issuers sometimes grant a lower rate to retain a reliable customer, particularly if you mention a competing offer.

Does carrying a small balance help my credit score?

No — this is a common myth. You don't need to carry a balance or pay interest to build credit. Paying in full every month still reports your on-time payments and card usage to the credit bureaus.
