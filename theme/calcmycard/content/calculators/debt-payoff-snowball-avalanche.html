Quick Answer

The debt avalanche method (highest APR first) usually minimizes total interest. The debt snowball method (smallest balance first) often feels easier to stick with because it delivers quick wins. The calculator above runs both against your cards so you can compare estimated time and interest.

If you're carrying balances on more than one card, the order you pay them off in can change your total cost by hundreds or thousands of dollars. This calculator runs the two most popular strategies against your own cards.

How the Calculator Works

Enter each card's balance, APR, and minimum payment, plus one extra amount you can put toward debt each month. For both strategies, the calculator simulates each month in the same order:

  1. Interest is added to every open card (APR ÷ 12).
  2. The minimum payment is paid on every open card.
  3. Everything left of your monthly budget goes to one target card: the smallest balance (snowball) or the highest APR (avalanche).
  4. When a card reaches zero, its old minimum “rolls over” to the next target, because your total monthly budget (all starting minimums + extra) stays the same.

It reports total months, total interest, and the order and month each card is paid off.

Formula

Monthly Budget = sum of every card's starting minimum + Extra (stays fixed)
Each open card: Balance = Balance + Balance × (APR ÷ 12) − Minimum
Target card(s): Balance = Balance − (Budget − minimums paid this month)
Snowball target = lowest balance  |  Avalanche target = highest APR (ties: the order you entered the cards)

This is a simplified estimate, not an issuer-specific statement calculation. If the budget left over is more than the target card owes, the rest goes to the next target in the same month.

Which One Should You Use?

If the interest difference for your cards is small, pick the method you're most likely to stick with — a plan you abandon saves nothing. If the avalanche saves a meaningful amount, it's usually worth the extra discipline.

When the Result May Differ

Treat the results as a planning estimate. Your real numbers can differ because of:

  • Daily periodic rate: many issuers apply APR ÷ 365 (or 360) each day instead of APR ÷ 12 once a month, which can produce different interest.
  • Average daily balance: your balance moves during the cycle as purchases post and payments arrive; this calculator uses a single month-start balance.
  • Fees: annual, late, foreign-transaction, cash-advance, and balance-transfer fees aren't included unless the calculator asks for them.
  • New purchases: the estimate assumes you stop adding charges. Any new spending lengthens payoff and raises interest.
  • Different APR categories: purchases, balance transfers, cash advances, and penalty APRs can carry different rates on the same card, and how payments are allocated between them affects the total.
  • Not keeping the budget fixed: the estimate assumes you keep paying the same total each month after a card is cleared. Spending the freed-up money instead slows both methods down.
  • Changing minimums: real minimum payments usually fall as balances fall; this calculator keeps each card's minimum fixed.
  • Promotional rates: a card with a 0% promo would change which card the avalanche targets.
