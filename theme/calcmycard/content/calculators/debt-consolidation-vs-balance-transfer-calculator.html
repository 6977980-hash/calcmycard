Quick Answer

A 0% balance transfer is often cheapest if you can pay off the balance within the promotional period. A fixed-rate consolidation loan can be better for larger balances or longer timelines because its rate and payment don't change. The calculator above compares estimated total costs side by side.

Two tools solve the same problem — a high-interest card balance — in different ways. A balance transfer moves the debt to a card with a temporary low rate; a consolidation loan replaces it with a fixed-rate, fixed-term installment loan.

How the Calculator Works

Enter the balance, the transfer offer (fee, promo APR, promo months, go-to APR), and the loan terms (APR, term in months, origination fee).

  • Balance transfer: adds the fee to the balance and assumes a flat payment of (balance + fee) ÷ promo months, then simulates payoff with the monthly model.
  • Consolidation loan: adds the origination fee to the amount borrowed, calculates a fixed installment payment, and totals interest and fees over the term.

It reports the total cost of each, the loan's monthly payment, and which option costs less.

Formula

Transfer Cost = Transfer Fee + Interest (promo APR, then go-to APR, APR ÷ 12)
Transfer Payment = (Balance + Fee) ÷ Promo Months
Loan Payment = L × r ÷ [1 − (1 + r)−n], where L = balance + origination fee, r = APR ÷ 12, n = months
Loan Cost = Loan Payment × n − Balance

This is a simplified estimate, not an issuer-specific statement calculation. The loan formula is the standard installment-loan formula; your lender's actual payment may differ.

If you can afford about $458 a month, the transfer is far cheaper here. If you can only manage about $275, the loan's fixed payment may be more realistic — and at that payment the transfer would leave a balance at the go-to APR once the promo ends.

Credit Score Effects Differ Too

A balance transfer keeps the debt as revolving credit, which still counts toward utilization. A consolidation loan turns it into installment debt, which scoring models treat differently and can lower reported card utilization. Both usually involve a hard inquiry when you apply.

When the Result May Differ

Treat the results as a planning estimate. Your real numbers can differ because of:

  • Daily periodic rate: many issuers apply APR ÷ 365 (or 360) each day instead of APR ÷ 12 once a month, which can produce different interest.
  • Fees: annual, late, foreign-transaction, cash-advance, and balance-transfer fees aren't included unless the calculator asks for them.
  • New purchases: the estimate assumes you stop adding charges. Any new spending lengthens payoff and raises interest.
  • Different APR categories: purchases, balance transfers, cash advances, and penalty APRs can carry different rates on the same card, and how payments are allocated between them affects the total.
  • Approval and terms: the APR, fee, and credit limit or loan amount you're offered depend on your credit profile and may differ from the inputs.
  • How origination fees are charged: some lenders deduct the fee from the loan proceeds instead of adding it to the amount borrowed.
  • Promo rules: paying more than 60 days late can let an issuer end a promotional rate early, and deferred-interest offers work differently from true 0% APR.
  • Running up the old cards: both options only help if the paid-off cards don't collect new balances.
