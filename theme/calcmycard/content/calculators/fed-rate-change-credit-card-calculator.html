Quick Answer

Most credit cards have a variable APR set at the prime rate plus a margin, and the prime rate moves by the same amount as the Fed's target rate. So a 0.25-point Fed hike or cut moves your card APR by 0.25 point, usually within one or two billing cycles. On a $5,000 balance that is about $1.04 a month in interest. Over the life of the balance, paying $200 a month at 22.15% APR, it adds or saves about $30.

Every Fed meeting brings headlines about what a rate change means for your wallet. For a credit card, the answer is simple math, and this calculator does it with your own balance, APR, and payment.

How a Fed Decision Reaches Your Credit Card

  1. The Fed sets the federal funds target. As of its September 16, 2026 meeting, the target range is 3.75% to 4.00%, after a 0.25-point hike.
  2. Banks move the prime rate the same amount, usually the next day. By convention the prime rate is the top of the Fed's range plus 3 points, so it is now 7.00% (since September 17, 2026).
  3. Your variable APR follows the prime rate. Your cardholder agreement sets your APR as prime plus a margin, for example prime + 15.15% = 22.15%. Issuers don't have to send advance notice when an index change moves your rate, so the new APR simply shows up on a statement, usually within one or two billing cycles.

Your margin doesn't change with the Fed. Only the prime rate part moves.

Formula

New APR = Current APR + Fed change
Change in monthly interest = Balance × Fed change ÷ 12

For a variable-rate card. The calculator then runs your payoff month by month at both APRs (APR ÷ 12 each month, same payment) to compare total interest and payoff time.

The Fed moves the rate a quarter point at a time, so one decision changes this card's cost by about a dollar a month. Your own choices move it much more: paying $210 a month instead of $200 at the higher 22.40% APR costs $1,674 in total interest, less than the $1,768 that $200 a month costs at the old 22.15%. Ten extra dollars a month more than cancels a hike.

When the Result May Differ

  • Fixed APR: a fixed-rate card doesn't follow the Fed. Your issuer can still raise the rate on new purchases after 45 days' notice.
  • Promotional 0% APR: the intro rate stays fixed until it ends; the Fed change affects the rate that applies afterward. Plan it with the 0% Intro APR Calculator.
  • Timing: your APR changes on the date set in your agreement, often the first day of a billing cycle after the prime rate moves.
  • Daily interest: issuers charge daily interest on your average daily balance, so your statement may differ by a few cents from this monthly estimate.
  • Rate floors: some agreements set a minimum APR that a Fed cut can't push below.
