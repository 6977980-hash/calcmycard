Quick Answer

Adding even a small fixed amount to your monthly credit card payment can cut months or years off payoff and reduce total interest. The calculator above compares your current payment with a higher one and estimates the months and interest saved.

Paying more than you have to, even by a modest amount, has an outsized effect on how much interest you pay and how fast you're debt-free. This calculator estimates how much, using your own balance and rate. To first see how much interest your current payment covers, start with the Credit Card Interest Calculator.

How the Calculator Works

Enter your balance, APR, current monthly payment, and the extra amount you'd add each month. The calculator runs two month-by-month simulations — one at your current payment and one with the extra amount — using the monthly model (APR ÷ 12). It reports:

  • Payoff time for both, and the months saved
  • Total interest for both, and the interest saved
  • A table comparing several common extra amounts side by side

Formula

Each month: Interest = Balance × (APR ÷ 12)
Balance = Balance + Interest − (Payment + Extra)
Interest Saved = Total Interest (current) − Total Interest (with extra)
Months Saved = Months (current) − Months (with extra)

This is a simplified estimate, not an issuer-specific statement calculation.

Why Extra Payments Punch Above Their Weight

Every extra dollar goes straight to principal, so it stops generating interest for every remaining month of the payoff. The earlier you add it, the more interest it prevents, because it reduces the largest balance you'll carry.

When the Result May Differ

Treat the results as a planning estimate. Your real numbers can differ because of:

  • Daily periodic rate: many issuers apply APR ÷ 365 (or 360) each day instead of APR ÷ 12 once a month, which can produce different interest.
  • Average daily balance: your balance moves during the cycle as purchases post and payments arrive; this calculator uses a single month-start balance.
  • Fees: annual, late, foreign-transaction, cash-advance, and balance-transfer fees aren't included unless the calculator asks for them.
  • New purchases: the estimate assumes you stop adding charges. Any new spending lengthens payoff and raises interest.
  • Grace period: whether new purchases accrue interest depends on whether you pay your statement balance in full and on your card's terms.
  • Different APR categories: purchases, balance transfers, cash advances, and penalty APRs can carry different rates on the same card, and how payments are allocated between them affects the total.
  • Timing within the month: on cards that use an average daily balance, paying earlier in the cycle can save slightly more than the estimate; consistency matters more than timing.
  • Payment allocation: if you have balances at different APRs, federal rules generally direct amounts above the minimum to the highest-APR balance first, which can change the savings.
