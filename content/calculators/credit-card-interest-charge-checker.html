Quick Answer

To check the interest charge on your credit card statement, multiply your average daily balance by your APR ÷ 365 and by the days in the billing cycle. For example, a $2,033.33 average daily balance at 24.99% APR over 30 days should cost about $41.76. If your charge is much higher, a higher-APR balance, a lost grace period, or trailing interest is the usual reason.

Most people never check the interest line on their statement. This checker takes the numbers printed on it and tells you whether the charge matches your APR — and, if it doesn't, the most likely reasons.

How the Checker Works

Enter the interest charged, your average daily balance, your purchase APR, and the days in the billing cycle (all usually printed in the "Interest Charge Calculation" section of your statement). The checker then:

  • Works out the interest the daily periodic rate method predicts.
  • Shows the gap between that and your actual charge.
  • Backs out the APR your charge implies, so you can compare it with the rate on your statement.

Formula

Expected interest = Average daily balance × (APR ÷ 365) × Days in cycle
Implied APR = Interest charged ÷ (Average daily balance × Days in cycle) × 365

Uses simple daily interest. Issuers that compound daily charge slightly more, so a gap of a few cents to about 3% is normal.

Example

A statement shows a $2,033.33 average daily balance, a 24.99% purchase APR, a 30-day cycle, and a $41.76 interest charge. Expected interest is $2,033.33 × 0.0685% × 30 ≈ $41.76, so the charge matches. If the same statement showed $55.00, the implied APR would be about 32.9%, a sign that part of the balance carries a higher rate, such as a cash advance.

Why Your Interest Charge Can Be Higher

  • Several balances, several APRs: cash advances and some balance transfers carry their own, often higher, APR. Statements list each balance type separately.
  • Lost grace period: if you didn't pay the previous statement balance in full, new purchases usually accrue interest from the day they post. See how the grace period works.
  • Residual (trailing) interest: interest that built up between your statement date and the day your payoff arrived can appear on the next statement, even after you paid the balance.
  • Penalty APR or an ended promotion: a late payment or the end of a 0% offer can raise the rate.
  • Minimum interest charge: some cards charge a small minimum (often $1 to $2) whenever any interest is due.

When the Result May Differ

The checker can't see the day-by-day balances behind your average, how your payments were allocated across balances, or fees included in the balance. Your statement and cardholder agreement are the final word; if a charge still doesn't add up, ask your issuer to walk you through it.
