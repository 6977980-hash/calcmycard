Quick Answer

APR (annual percentage rate) is the yearly cost of carrying a balance. The calculator above turns your APR into estimated dollar costs — per day, per month, and per year — for the balance you enter.

"24.99% APR" doesn't mean much until you see it in dollars. This calculator translates the percentage on your statement into what carrying your balance could cost.

How the Calculator Works

Enter the balance you're carrying and your APR. The calculator shows:

  • Cost this month: balance × (APR ÷ 12).
  • Cost per day: balance × (APR ÷ 365), the daily-rate view many issuers use.
  • Cost per year (simple): balance × APR, with no compounding.
  • Cost per year (compounded monthly): what accumulates if the balance and its interest revolve untouched for 12 months.

It also shows a 12-month table of a balance left to revolve with no payments — a worst-case view of the cost of doing nothing.

Formula

Monthly Cost = Balance × (APR ÷ 12)
Daily Cost = Balance × (APR ÷ 365)
Annual Cost (simple) = Balance × APR
Annual Cost (compounded monthly) = Balance × [(1 + APR ÷ 12)12 − 1]

This is a simplified estimate, not an issuer-specific statement calculation.

Purchase APR vs. Cash Advance APR

Many cards list several APRs: one for purchases, often a higher one for cash advances, and sometimes a penalty APR after a late payment. Cash advances typically begin accruing interest immediately and generally don't receive the same grace period that applies to purchases; the Cash Advance Calculator adds up the fee and interest on one. For credit cards, APR and "interest rate" are usually the same number, unlike mortgages, where APR bundles in certain fees.

When the Result May Differ

Treat the results as a planning estimate. Your real numbers can differ because of:

  • Daily periodic rate: many issuers apply APR ÷ 365 (or 360) each day instead of APR ÷ 12 once a month, which can produce different interest.
  • Average daily balance: your balance moves during the cycle as purchases post and payments arrive; this calculator uses a single month-start balance.
  • Fees: annual, late, foreign-transaction, cash-advance, and balance-transfer fees aren't included unless the calculator asks for them.
  • New purchases: the estimate assumes you stop adding charges. Any new spending lengthens payoff and raises interest.
  • Grace period: whether new purchases accrue interest depends on whether you pay your statement balance in full and on your card's terms.
  • Different APR categories: purchases, balance transfers, cash advances, and penalty APRs can carry different rates on the same card, and how payments are allocated between them affects the total.
  • Variable rates: most card APRs are variable and can change when the benchmark rate (such as the prime rate) moves.
