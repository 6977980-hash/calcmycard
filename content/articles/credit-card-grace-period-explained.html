Quick Answer

A credit card grace period is the window between your billing cycle's end and your payment due date during which, on cards that offer one, no interest accrues on eligible purchases — generally only if you paid your previous statement balance in full. If you don't pay your statement balance in full, you may lose your grace period for new purchases, depending on your card's terms.

A grace period can help you avoid interest on eligible purchases when you pay your full statement balance by the due date.

How it actually works

Grace periods aren't universally required, and their length and rules depend on your card's terms. Federal rules (Regulation Z § 1026.5) require card issuers to mail or deliver your statement at least 21 days before the payment due date. For cards that offer a grace period, paying the full statement balance by the due date can help you avoid interest on eligible purchases, and on many cards the grace period continues each cycle you keep paying in full. Not every transaction gets the same treatment, and your cardholder agreement controls the actual terms.

The moment it disappears

If you don't pay your statement balance in full, you may lose your grace period for new purchases, depending on your card's terms. When that happens, new purchases can start accruing interest from the date of purchase until you pay in full again and requalify — which may take more than one billing cycle on some cards. Your cardholder agreement explains exactly how your card handles this. Interest that builds after your statement prints can also show up as a small charge on the next one; see how credit card interest works and statement balance vs. current balance vs. payoff amount.

Why this trips people up

A partial payment costs more than people expect, but not in the way many guides suggest. If you pay only part of your statement balance, you keep paying interest on the unpaid part, new purchases usually start building interest from the day you make them, and interest that built up before your payment posted can show up on the next statement as "trailing" interest. What the issuer can't do is charge interest on the part of the balance you repaid within the grace period, or reach back to balances from earlier billing cycles ("double-cycle billing"). Federal rules ban both (Regulation Z § 1026.54), and the CFPB explains the same grace period rules.

Cash advances usually don't get a grace period

Cash advances typically begin accruing interest immediately and generally don't receive the same grace period that applies to purchases. Balance transfers are often treated similarly unless a promotional rate applies. Cash advances also commonly carry a higher APR and a separate fee, which is why they're usually one of the most expensive ways to use a credit card (our Cash Advance Calculator shows the full cost). Check your card's terms for the specifics.

The one habit that protects your grace period

Set autopay to your full statement balance, not the minimum payment. This single setting helps prevent one of the most common ways people accidentally lose a grace period: forgetting, or assuming the minimum payment is "good enough." Frequently Asked Questions

Do all credit cards offer a grace period?

Most do, but card issuers aren't required to offer one, and the length and rules vary by card. Check your cardholder agreement for your card's specific grace period terms.

If I pay my balance in full one month, do I automatically get a grace period the next?

Not always. Paying your full statement balance by the due date maintains the grace period on cards that offer one, but if you'd already lost it, some cards require one or more consecutive full payments before it's restored. Check your cardholder agreement for your card's rule.
