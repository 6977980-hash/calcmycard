Quick Answer

A credit card late fee is charged when your minimum payment doesn't arrive by the due date. Federal rules cap it: the fee can't be more than the minimum payment that was due, only one fee can be charged per late payment, and the usual ceiling in 2026 is the inflation-adjusted "safe harbor": $30 for a first late payment and $41 for another late payment within the next six billing cycles. The CFPB's planned $8 cap never took effect; a federal court vacated it on April 15, 2025.

A late fee is the cost you see first, but it is rarely the only one. Depending on how late the payment is, it can also cost you your grace period, a mark on your credit report, a penalty APR, and on some promotions all the deferred interest. Here is what each stage costs and how to avoid it.

How much is a credit card late fee?

Your cardholder agreement and the "Late payment" line near the due date on every statement show your card's fee. Regulation Z § 1026.52(b) limits what an issuer can charge:

  • A safe-harbor amount. An issuer can charge up to a set dollar amount without having to justify its costs. For 2026 the safe harbor is $30 for a first late payment and $41 for another late payment in the next six billing cycles. Those amounts took effect on January 1, 2022 (Federal Register, November 2, 2021). The CFPB is supposed to review them each year for inflation, but its annual adjustment rules since then, including the one for 2026, haven't changed them. A higher fee is allowed only if the issuer can show its costs justify it.
  • No more than the minimum payment. The fee can't exceed the dollar amount of the payment you missed. If your minimum was $25, the late fee can be no more than $25.
  • One fee per late payment. A single late payment can't bring two penalty fees, such as a late fee and a returned-payment fee for the same event.

What happened to the $8 late fee cap?

In March 2024 the CFPB finalized a rule that would have cut the safe harbor to $8 for the largest card issuers. A federal court in Texas blocked it before its May 2024 start date, and on April 15, 2025 the court vacated the rule at the joint request of the CFPB and the industry groups that had sued. Card issuers never had to apply the $8 cap, and the inflation-adjusted safe harbor above still applies.

When does a payment count as late?

  • The same due date each month. Your due date must fall on the same day every month, and your statement must arrive at least 21 days before it (Regulation Z § 1026.5).
  • Not before 5 p.m. An issuer can set a cutoff time on the due date, but not earlier than 5 p.m. (Regulation Z § 1026.10).
  • Weekends and holidays. If the due date falls on a day the issuer doesn't accept mailed payments, a payment received the next business day isn't late.
  • Paying less than the minimum is late too. A partial payment below the minimum can still bring the fee.

What else a late payment can cost

Typical consequences by how late the minimum payment is. Your card's terms decide the details.
How lateWhat can happen
1 day or moreA late fee. If you also don't pay the full statement balance, you lose the grace period and new purchases start accruing interest.
30 days or moreThe issuer can report the late payment to the credit bureaus. It can stay on your credit report for up to seven years.
60 days or moreA penalty APR can apply to your existing balance, and promotional rates on existing balances can end (Regulation Z § 1026.55). On a deferred-interest plan, the CFPB notes this can trigger the deferred interest.

Before 60 days, a penalty APR can apply only to new transactions, after 45 days' notice. To see what a higher rate costs: on a $3,000 balance, monthly interest is about $62.48 at 24.99% and about $74.98 at a 29.99% penalty APR, or roughly $12.50 more every month. Try your own numbers in the APR Calculator. If a penalty APR is applied to your existing balance because you were more than 60 days late, the issuer must lower it again once you make six on-time minimum payments in a row.

If a late payment hits a deferred-interest promotion, the cost can be far larger than the fee. The Deferred Interest Calculator shows how much interest is building up behind a "no interest if paid in full" offer.

How to get a late fee waived

If you're rarely late, call the number on the back of your card soon after the payment posts and ask. Many issuers will waive a first late fee as a courtesy, especially if you pay right away; it isn't guaranteed, and it's harder to get after repeated late payments. Ask whether the late payment will be reported, too: one that is fewer than 30 days late normally isn't.

How to avoid late fees

  • Set autopay for at least the minimum. Then pay more by hand each month. Autopay for the minimum alone keeps the fee away but not the interest; use the Minimum Payment Calculator to see what minimums cost over time.
  • Turn on due-date alerts by text or email in your card's app.
  • Move your due date. Many issuers let you pick a due date that falls just after payday.
  • Pay a few days early. Bank transfers can take a day or two to post, and mailed checks longer.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does one late payment hurt my credit score?

A payment that is fewer than 30 days late usually isn't reported to the credit bureaus, so it costs you the fee but not your score. Once it is 30 or more days late and reported, it can lower your score and stay on your report for up to seven years.

Can a late fee be higher than my minimum payment?

No. Under Regulation Z § 1026.52(b), a late fee can't exceed the minimum payment that was due. If your minimum was $15, the fee can be no more than $15.

Is there still an $8 late fee cap?

No. The CFPB's 2024 rule that would have set an $8 cap for large issuers was blocked before it took effect and was vacated by a federal court on April 15, 2025.

Do I get charged interest on top of the late fee?

Often, yes. If you don't pay the full statement balance by the due date, interest is charged on the balance you carry, and new purchases lose their grace period until you pay in full again. See How to Avoid Credit Card Interest.
