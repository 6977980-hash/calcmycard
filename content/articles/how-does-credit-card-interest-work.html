Quick Answer

Many card issuers calculate interest using a daily periodic rate (your APR divided by 365, or sometimes 360), applied to your average daily balance and added up over your billing cycle. For cards that offer a grace period, paying the full statement balance by the due date can help you avoid interest on eligible purchases. Cash advances and balance transfers may be treated differently, and your cardholder agreement sets the actual terms.

Most people know credit cards "charge interest," but far fewer know when that interest starts, when it stops, and why a statement can show interest even after a balance is paid. Here's how it works, in plain English.

The short version: a daily rate on your average balance

Your Annual Percentage Rate (APR) is a yearly rate, but issuers don't apply it once a year. They turn it into a small daily rate (APR ÷ 365, or 360 for a few issuers) and apply it to your balance every day of the billing cycle, usually through the average daily balance. The day-by-day charges are added up and billed once a month on your statement. For the formula and a worked example with purchases and payments mid-cycle, see our step-by-step guide on how to calculate credit card interest.

Is interest charged daily or monthly?

Both, in a sense: many issuers calculate interest every day using the daily periodic rate, then bill the total once a month on your statement. Many compound daily, so each day's interest is added to the balance and earns interest itself the next day. Over a single month that adds only cents, but over years of carrying a balance it adds up.

When interest starts

  • Purchases: usually no interest if you paid your previous statement balance in full and pay the new one by the due date (the grace period). Otherwise, interest typically starts from the purchase date.
  • Cash advances: interest usually starts the day you take the cash, with no grace period, often at a higher APR plus a fee. See what one costs with the Cash Advance Calculator.
  • Balance transfers: interest typically applies from the transfer date unless a promotional rate covers it.

Why "average daily balance" matters more than people think

Your balance moves around all month as you spend and make payments, so issuers don't just look at a single snapshot — they average it across every day of the cycle. This means paying down a balance earlier in the month (rather than waiting until the due date) genuinely lowers your interest charge, because it lowers your average for more days.

The one rule that can make interest disappear entirely

Most cards offer a grace period on purchases (card issuers aren't required to, according to the CFPB): if you pay your entire statement balance by the due date, you owe no interest on that cycle's purchases. The full set of habits that keep it that way is in our guide on how to avoid credit card interest.

When interest stops: losing and getting back the grace period

If you pay less than the full statement balance, you usually lose the grace period. From then on, new purchases can start building interest from the day you make them, not from the due date. Interest keeps running until the balance is paid in full.

Getting the grace period back usually takes paying the full statement balance by the due date, and some cards require you to do that for two cycles in a row. Your cardholder agreement says which rule your card uses, so one partial payment can cost more than the interest on the unpaid amount alone.

There are limits, though. When you lose the grace period, the issuer can't charge interest on the part of the balance you repaid within the grace period, and it can't charge interest on balances from earlier billing cycles ("double-cycle billing"). Both are banned by Regulation Z § 1026.54.

Residual interest: why a paid-off balance can still show a charge

When you pay off a balance that was already building interest, the statement you paid from was printed days or weeks earlier. Interest kept adding up between the statement date and the day your payment posted. That leftover amount, often called residual or trailing interest, can show up on the next statement even though you paid the "full" balance you were shown. Paying that small charge in full by its due date usually ends it, and asking your issuer for a payoff amount avoids it altogether; see statement balance vs. current balance vs. payoff amount.

Different balances, different APRs

One card can hold several balances, each with its own APR: purchases, a balance transfer at a promotional rate, and a cash advance at a higher rate. Interest is worked out separately on each one. Your minimum payment can go to any of them, but under Regulation Z § 1026.53 anything you pay above the minimum must go to the balance with the highest APR first. That is why paying more than the minimum matters most on a card that also carries a cash advance.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does interest get charged if I pay in full every month?

Usually not on purchases. For cards that offer a grace period, paying the full statement balance by the due date can help you avoid interest on eligible purchases. Cash advances and some balance transfers can accrue interest right away, so check your cardholder agreement for your card's terms.

Is interest charged on my whole balance or just what I didn't pay?

Mostly on the unpaid part. After a partial payment you pay interest on what's left, new purchases usually start building interest right away, and some trailing interest can appear on the next statement. Federal rules (Regulation Z § 1026.54) stop issuers from charging interest on the part you repaid within the grace period, and ban double-cycle billing. That's why partial payments can still cost more than people expect, even with those limits.

Why did my interest charge go up even though I paid the same amount?

If your balance (or your average daily balance) increased due to new purchases, your interest charge increases proportionally, even with an unchanged payment amount.
